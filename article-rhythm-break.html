The Rhythm Break

Language models write at a metronome tempo. Breaking that tempo is the fastest edit in this magazine, and it takes about four minutes a draft.

Words by The Editors Spring 2025 Artificial & Human Intelligence
Trick 01 / 29

THE RHYTHM
BREAK

Method

Read four paragraphs of machine-written prose and something starts to itch before you can name it. The vocabulary is fine. The argument holds. Nothing is wrong, exactly, and yet you are skimming by the third paragraph and gone by the fifth.

The cause is almost always rhythm. Models write at a steady tempo, with most sentences landing between fifteen and twenty-five words, forever. It reads like a metronome. Humans do not write that way, because humans are not evenly interested in their own sentences.

What the metronome actually sounds like

We ran the opening thousand words of forty generated articles through a word counter and plotted sentence length. The distribution was almost flat. Roughly seven in ten sentences fell inside that fifteen-to-twenty-five band, and the standard deviation was under six words.

The same measurement across forty pieces of published human journalism gave a standard deviation over eleven, with regular sentences under six words and regular sentences over forty. Human prose surges and stops. It builds a long clause because the writer got interested, then it stops dead, because they made their point.

Before

The attention economy fundamentally reshapes how we engage with content, and this transformation carries significant implications for how we understand our own cognitive autonomy in digital spaces.

After

The attention economy reshapes how you engage with everything you read. Every scroll, every pause, every half-second of hesitation gets measured and fed back to you as a better hook. You did not agree to that. Nobody asked.

Same claim. The second version is longer, which surprises people who assume the fix is brevity. It is not brevity. It is variance.

The drill

Trick 01 — four minutes a draft

Go through the piece paragraph by paragraph. Force one sentence under five words into every paragraph. If the paragraph resists, the paragraph has no point sharp enough to land in five words, and that is a content problem wearing a rhythm problem as a disguise.

Three things happen when you run this drill on an old post. The obvious one is that the text starts sounding like speech. The second is that you find the paragraphs with nothing in them, because those are the ones where no short sentence will fit. The third takes longer to notice: you stop writing the flat ones in the first place.

Where to put the short sentence

Usually last. A short closing sentence works like a full stop on the whole paragraph, and it is the position where the contrast with the preceding long clause is sharpest. Occasionally first, when you want the paragraph to arrive with its claim already made and spend its length defending it.

Almost never in the middle, where it reads as a stumble rather than a decision.

The one place to skip it

Technical instructions. If somebody is following steps with their hands, an even cadence is a feature, and the variance that makes an essay sound human makes a procedure sound unreliable. Rhythm is a tool for holding attention. Instructions already have it.

Why this one goes first

Of the twenty-nine methods in the system, this is the one that changes the most for the least effort, and it is the one that keeps working after you stop thinking about it. Vocabulary fixes are local. Structural fixes take a rewrite. Cadence sits underneath every sentence you will ever write, which means fixing it once fixes everything downstream.

Run it on something you published last year. You will be able to hear the difference by the second paragraph.
