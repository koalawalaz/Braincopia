Abstraction Is Where Voice Goes to Die

Meaningful engagement. Significant implications. Every abstract noun you keep is a place your reader could have met you and did not.

Words by The Editors 29 July Issue 01
Trick 07 / 29

KILL THE
ABSTRACTION

Voice & writing

Here is a sentence that means nothing: "Our approach delivers meaningful improvements in team engagement." Read it twice and you still cannot say what happened, to whom, or how anybody would know.

Now here is the same claim with an object attached: "Two of the four people who used to skip our Monday call started showing up." That one you can picture. You can also argue with it, which is the tell that it is actually saying something.

The abstraction ladder, and which rung to stand on

Every noun sits somewhere on a ladder. At the top: engagement. Below that: attendance. Below that: the Monday call. At the bottom: the two people who used to skip it.

Writing that reads as generated lives near the top of that ladder almost exclusively, and for a structural reason rather than a stylistic one. A model producing text about your situation does not have your Monday call. It has the general shape of situations like yours, and the general shape is the abstract rung. So it writes there, fluently, and the fluency is what makes the emptiness hard to spot.

The abstract rung is where every version of this sentence is identical. The bottom rung is where only your version exists.Law 03, the Five Laws of Braincopia

The swap

Trick 07 — ten minutes a draft

Underline every abstract noun in your draft: engagement, impact, transformation, framework, solution, experience. For each one, ask what you actually saw. Replace the noun with the thing you saw. If you cannot name anything you saw, delete the sentence, because you did not know anything when you wrote it.

Most drafts lose between a fifth and a third of their length to this pass, which alarms people. It should not. The deleted sentences were the ones carrying no information, and their only function was to make the piece feel substantial while you were writing it.

The three that hide best

Experience. Almost always replaceable with the specific thing somebody did. "Improve the onboarding experience" means "stop asking for the phone number twice."

Solution. A word people reach for when naming the actual thing would reveal how small it is. Name it anyway. Small and real beats large and vague, every time, in every register.

Impact. Usually a number wearing a costume. If you have the number, use it. If you do not have the number, you do not have the impact, and you should write the sentence you can actually support.

The objection

Somebody always says that abstraction is how you generalise, and that a piece made entirely of specifics cannot make an argument. True, and not the point. The structure most writing needs is one abstract claim supported by concrete detail, in roughly that ratio.

What fails is the inversion: abstract claim, abstract support, abstract restatement. That is three rungs of ladder with nobody standing on any of them, and it is what most professional writing has quietly become.

Put one thing you actually saw into every paragraph. That is the whole method, and it is unfakeable, because nobody else was there.
