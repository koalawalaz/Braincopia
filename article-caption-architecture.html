Caption Architecture

A caption is a building with three floors. Most people write the lobby, skip the stairs, and wonder why nobody reaches the top.

Words by The Editors 24 July Issue 01
Discipline 04

CAPTION
ARCHITECTURE

Hook, body, ask

A caption is a building with three floors, and most people write the lobby, skip the stairs, and then wonder why nobody reaches the top.

The three floors are the hook, the body, and the ask. Each has a job, each fails in a characteristic way, and the failures compound, because a reader lost on the first floor never finds out that the second one was good.

Floor one: the hook

The hook has exactly one job, and it is not to be interesting. It is to make the next line unavoidable. Those are different objectives and confusing them is why so many hooks are clever and dead.

The reliable shapes are narrow. A specific number with the context withheld. A flat statement that contradicts what the reader believes. A concrete moment dropped in without setup. What does not work is a question the reader can answer with "no" and scroll on, which is most questions.

Length matters more than people expect. Under about eight words the hook has no time to create a gap. Over about eighteen it stops being a hook and starts being the first sentence of the body. Somewhere between is where the good ones sit.

Floor two: the body

The body is where captions die, and they die of two things.

The first is a wall of text with no line breaks, which loses a reader physically before it loses them intellectually. Break every one to three sentences. This looks wasteful on a screen and reads well on a phone, and the phone is where you are being read.

The second is the tempo problem covered in The Rhythm Break. Captions are the format where a flat cadence is most fatal, because the reader has not committed to anything and the cost of leaving is zero. Vary sentence length hard here, harder than you would in an essay.

The one-detail rule

Every caption body carries at least one specific thing only you could report. The client who paid in three instalments. The version that shipped with the wrong font. Without it, the caption is advice, and advice is the most abundant substance on the internet.

Floor three: the ask

One ask. Not two, and never a menu.

The common failure is asking for engagement instead of asking for the thing you want. "Let me know your thoughts below" is not an ask. It is a person who does not want to seem like they are selling, and readers can hear that hesitation clearly.

Say the actual thing. "The full set is in the link." "Reply with the one you would cut." "Save this for the next time you are stuck at the top of a draft." Specific asks get acted on and vague ones get scrolled past, which is the same rule as everything else in this magazine, applied to the last line instead of the first.

The load-bearing wall

All three floors rest on one thing: the caption sounds like a person who was there. Structure will not save a caption with nothing in it, and no hook has ever rescued a body that could have been written by anybody about anything.

Build the floors. Then put something of yours on each one.
