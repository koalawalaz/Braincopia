The Algorithm Does Not Steal Your Time

It quietly re-architects your boredom. What the attention economy takes is not your hours, it is the self that used to wander.

Words by The Editors Spring 2025 Neuroscience & Philosophy of Mind
World V / Mind

COLONISED
INTERIORITY

Attention & the self

The standard complaint about the attention economy is that it takes your time. Hours vanish, the argument goes, and you get nothing back but a vague film of dissatisfaction.

That framing is too generous. Time is recoverable. You can put the phone in a drawer and get the hours back tomorrow. What the feed takes is not stored in hours at all.

The thing that actually goes missing

Consider what used to happen in the gaps. Waiting for a kettle, standing on a platform, lying awake at two in the morning. Those intervals were not empty. They were where the mind went wandering without supervision, and a startling proportion of what anybody has ever thought was thought in one of them.

The feed did not fill those gaps. It removed the conditions that produced them. Boredom, the state that used to precede wandering, has been quietly redesigned into a state that precedes refreshing.

The algorithm does not steal your time. It quietly re-architects your boredom, until the self that used to wander, wait, and wonder has been replaced by a self that only refreshes.Braincopia, Vol. I

This is not distraction. Distraction is a temporary condition with a known cure. This is a redesign of interiority, and it does not announce itself, because the replacement state feels exactly like the original one from the inside.

Why it is invisible

Every measurement the industry uses is a measurement of behaviour: sessions, dwell time, retention. All of them are external. None of them can see the thing being changed, because the thing being changed is the felt quality of an unoccupied mind, and no dashboard has ever had access to that.

You cannot notice the absence of a thought you never had. The wandering that did not happen leaves no trace, files no complaint, and shows up in no metric. It is the perfect loss: total, continuous, and undetectable by anyone including the person losing it.

The test

Stand somewhere with nothing to do and no device for four minutes. Not as a wellness exercise. As an instrument. What you are measuring is how long it takes before the reach for the phone arrives, and whether anything at all shows up in the interval before it does.

The part nobody agreed to

The usual defence is consent. You downloaded the application. You accepted the terms. Nobody made you scroll.

But consent covers the transaction, not the consequence. A person can agree to be shown things and still not have agreed to have the shape of their own idle mind rebuilt around the showing. Every scroll, every pause, every half-second of hesitation is measured and returned to you as a better hook. That loop was not in the terms, because it cannot be written into terms. It is an emergent property of optimisation, and optimisation does not require anybody's permission to work.

What this magazine does about it

Nothing dramatic, and no advice about screen time. The response Braincopia has settled on is structural rather than behavioural: we publish long, we publish things that require a second reading, and we refuse the formats engineered to be consumed without residue.

A piece that can be understood at a glance leaves nothing behind. A piece that takes eleven minutes and asks something of you occupies the same space the wandering used to occupy, and it is the only substitute we have found that works.

The self that used to wander is not gone. It is under-employed. It responds immediately to being given something difficult, which is the closest thing to good news in this entire argument.
