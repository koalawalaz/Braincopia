The Heart That Beats on a Graph

The smartwatch does not track your heart. It replaces your heartbeat's meaning. A first report from Vol. II.

Words by The Editors Spring 2025 Surreal Fashion & Extreme Design
World I / Vol. II preview

THE BODY
ELECTRIC

Measured vs lived

The body was, for most of human history, the place where meaning was felt before it could be said. You knew you were afraid before you had the word. You knew something was wrong in a room before you could name what.

The smartwatch makes that legible. And in making it legible, it makes it slightly less yours.

Two bodies, one wrist

There is the lived body, the one you experience from the inside, and there is the measured body, the one that appears on a graph. They are not the same object, and the gap between them is where this entire subject lives.

The lived body reports in qualities: heavy, restless, wrong, fine. The measured body reports in quantities: sixty-two, four hours twelve minutes, eighty-one percent. Both are true. Only one of them can be shown to a doctor, sold to an insurer, or compared against last week, and so only one of them accumulates authority.

The smartwatch does not track your heart. It replaces your heartbeat’s meaning.Braincopia, Vol. II — The Body Electric

The quantified self does not know itself better

The promise of the wearable was self-knowledge. More data, better decisions, a clearer picture of the organism you happen to be operating.

What actually happens is subtler and stranger. The quantified self is not a self that knows itself better. It is a self that has outsourced its felt sense to a dashboard, and then learned to distrust the felt sense when the two disagree.

Ask anyone who wears a sleep tracker. They will tell you about mornings when they woke feeling fine, checked the score, saw it was bad, and felt worse for the rest of the day. The reverse happens too. A terrible night gets overruled by a good number and the person carries on. In both cases the arbitration went to the graph.

Fashion knew first

This is why the interesting work on wearables is not happening in health technology. It is happening in couture, where designers have understood for a century that anything placed on the body makes an argument about the body.

Biometric garments that display the wearer's state to the room. Pieces that deliberately misreport. Collections built around sensors that measure something meaningless with total precision, as a way of asking why precision reads as truth. This is not novelty design. It is the only serious criticism of the wearable currently being produced, and it is being produced by people the technology industry does not read.

Vol. II — The Body Electric

The summer edition takes this apart across fashion, neuroscience and technology at once: biometric couture, the aesthetics of the extended self, and what happens to interiority when the body becomes an interface. This piece is the first report from that work.

What is lost in translation

None of this is an argument against measurement. Measurement finds the arrhythmia, catches the apnoea, and occasionally saves the life. That is not in dispute and it should not need saying.

The argument is narrower. A heart that beats on a graph has been translated, and something is always lost in translation. What is lost here is the private, wordless, first-person authority the body used to have over its own condition. That authority was not always accurate. It was, however, unmistakably yours, and it is being traded for accuracy at an exchange rate nobody has been asked to approve.

The body is becoming an interface. The question Vol. II asks is who it is an interface for.
