Seven Worlds, One Grammar

The inaugural issue is built from seven places that do not exist. Here is why they all look like they were photographed by the same person.

Words by The Editors 12 August Issue 01
Issue 01 / Cover

THE PARALLEL
UNIVERSE

Seven worlds

The brief for Issue 01 was one sentence long: build seven places that do not exist, then report from inside them as though the assignment were ordinary. No fantasy, no world-building appendix, no maps. Seven locations, treated with the same flat attention a travel desk would give a mid-sized airport.

We did that for a reason. A machine will describe any place you name, instantly, at length, and the description will be fine. What it cannot do is decide which seven places are worth the trip. That decision is the entire magazine, and inventing worlds was the cleanest way to isolate it.

The grammar came first

Before a word was written we set the visual rules, and we kept them in a text file rather than a mood board so nobody could quietly drift. Five rules. All of them restrictions.

  • One light source per world, and it is always wrong. Shadows fall in a direction the geometry does not support. Nobody notices consciously. Everybody feels it.
  • Colour is contaminated, never applied. Every image is pushed through the same violet and yellow duotone at partial strength, so the palette sits underneath the photograph rather than on top of it.
  • Scale breaks once per spread. One object is the wrong size relative to its neighbours. Only one. Two reads as a joke.
  • Type never floats. Every caption is anchored to a hard edge, and the edges are drawn, not implied by white space.
  • No horizon is level. Between one and three degrees. Enough to unsettle, not enough to look like a mistake.

Rules like these are unfashionable because they sound arbitrary. They are arbitrary. That is the point. A system that could have been derived from the content would have been derived by anyone with the same content, and then the issue would look like every other issue about this subject.

Constraint is the only reliable signature. Anyone can add. Almost nobody will agree, in advance and in writing, to what they will refuse.Editorial note, Issue 01 production file

The seven

The Waiting Room has chairs, a wall clock, and no door. Nobody in it is anxious. That is the feature. We wanted the specific boredom of software that is permanently almost ready, and boredom photographs badly, so the room is shot at chair height with a lens that flattens everything to the same importance.

The Archive of Unsent Things holds every draft nobody sent. Shelved, catalogued, temperature controlled. It is the quietest feature in the issue and the one two readers have written to us about at length, both times to say it made them go and delete something.

Second Language asks six writers what it feels like to be fluent in a register that is not theirs. This one is not invented. We put it in the middle of the issue on purpose, so that the reported piece would borrow the strangeness of the fabricated ones on either side, and the fabricated ones would borrow its weight.

The Copy of a Copy applies generational loss to taste rather than image data. What survives the fourth reproduction is structure. What goes is everything that made the first one worth reproducing.

Loud Quiet is a city where the only status signal is restraint, and it is the feature that eventually became the brief for Issue 03.

The Machine's Idea of a Garden is designed entirely from descriptions of gardens. Nothing in it is wrong. The paths are the right width, the planting is seasonally correct, the benches face the view. Nothing grows, because nothing was planted by anyone who would have to come back in March.

Home, Rebuilt From Memory closes the issue. Every detail correct, the whole thing wrong. Anyone who has tried to draw the house they grew up in already knows the exact failure being described.

What the issue is arguing

Read together, the seven worlds make one claim, and it survives being stated plainly. The scarce thing is no longer execution. Execution is now available to everyone at roughly the same quality for roughly no money. What remains scarce is a person willing to say that these seven, and not the other four hundred, are the ones worth building.

That is not a comfortable argument for anybody who has spent a decade getting good at execution. We know. Half the editorial meetings for this issue were about exactly that discomfort, and the garden feature is where it ended up.

The rest of the magazine, including every method we publish, is downstream of this. Voice is not a decoration you apply at the end. It is the accumulated record of what you chose not to do.
