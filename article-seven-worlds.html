The Seven Worlds

Braincopia is organised across seven interconnected territories. Here is the full topology, and the law that stops any of them standing alone.

Words by The Editors Spring 2025 The Crossroads
Vol. I / Cover

THE SEVEN
WORLDS

Universe map

A magazine that covers everything covers nothing. That is the standard objection, and it is usually right. The publications that try to be about culture, technology, design and ideas at once end up with a table of contents that reads like a search history.

So Braincopia is not organised by subject. It is organised by world. Seven of them, fixed, each with its own centre of gravity, and a rule that no piece may sit comfortably inside only one.

World I — Surreal Fashion and Extreme Design

Where garments become manifestos. Fashion as philosophy, as protest, as hallucination. We document the designers who dress the impossible, and we treat a collection the way another magazine would treat a policy paper.

This is the world most often mistaken for decoration. It is the opposite. Clothing is the only argument a person makes before speaking, which makes it the fastest-moving indicator of what a culture currently believes about bodies, status and machines.

World II — Artificial and Human Intelligence

The frontier territory where neural networks meet neurons. Reports from inside the algorithm, interviews with machines, essays on what thinking even means now.

Braincopia is an HI/AI publication, which means this world is not a beat we cover. It is the condition under which every other world is now being covered.

World III — Visionary Business and Strategy

Business intelligence for people who find conventional commerce intellectually unbearable. Market disruption as creative act. The economy as a design problem rather than a spreadsheet with feelings.

World IV — Culture, Media and the Zeitgeist

What the culture is whispering before it starts screaming. Deep reads on film, music, subcultures, digital movements, and the invisible forces shaping the collective mind. The pieces here are usually early, which means they are usually argued with first and agreed with later.

World V — Neuroscience and the Philosophy of Mind

The oldest questions with the newest tools. Consciousness, identity, perception, free will, interrogated with rigour and rendered with beauty. We refuse the choice between a paper that is correct and a page that is worth looking at.

World VI — Speculative Design and Visual Futures

Design fiction for civilisations not yet built. Concept architecture, future interfaces, the aesthetics of scenarios that have not happened yet but already feel inevitable. This world is where the magazine tests its own visual grammar, because a future you cannot picture is a future nobody will argue about.

World VII — The Crossroads

Marketing, persuasion, and the architecture of desire. The dark arts made visible. How desire is manufactured, how attention is harvested, how meaning is engineered at scale. We expose the machinery, and we teach you to build your own.

World VII is not a seventh subject sitting alongside six others. It is the collision point of all of them: where fashion influences business, where AI reshapes culture, where neuroscience decodes marketing, where philosophy challenges design. Every piece in the other six worlds eventually has to answer to it, because every idea that reaches anybody reached them through some architecture somebody built.

Fashion informs business. Neuroscience informs design. AI informs culture. Nothing is siloed here.Law III — The Cross-Domain Law

Why the topology matters

Most magazines file by department because departments are how the advertising was sold. The categories are a billing structure that readers have been trained to mistake for a map of reality.

Seven worlds with mandatory overlap produces a different kind of assignment. A piece on wearable technology cannot be filed under gadgets, because the interesting part is what happens to the felt sense of your own body when it becomes legible to a dashboard. That is World I, World II and World V at once, and no single-department magazine would have commissioned it in that shape.

The topology is not a taxonomy. It is a constraint that makes the cross-domain piece the default rather than the exception, and the cross-domain piece is the only kind we are interested in publishing.
