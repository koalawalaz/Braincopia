The Untouched Template Is the Tell

Readers judge your layout before they process a single word. Six deliberate moves that make a stock template stop looking like one.

Words by The Editors 05 August Issue 01
Discipline 02

UNTOUCHED
TEMPLATE

Canva & Affinity

Readers judge a layout before they process a word of it. That judgement takes a fraction of a second, it happens whether or not they know anything about design, and it is almost entirely about whether somebody made decisions.

Which is good news. It means you do not need taste, a design education, or a better tool. You need six moves, and all of them are subtractive or structural rather than decorative.

01 — Set your own margins

Every template ships with even padding on all four sides, because even padding is safe for any content that might get poured in. Your content is not any content. Widen the outside margin, tighten the inside one, and let the block sit off-centre. Asymmetry reads as a decision, and a decision reads as a person.

02 — Break the type pairing

Template pairings are chosen to offend nobody, which means they land in the same narrow band of tasteful neutrality as every other template. Replace the display face and keep the body face. One change, and the layout stops matching the six others in the feed that used the same file.

If you keep a brand kit, lock it before you start rather than after. Half the template look is a designer who changed the colours but never touched the fonts.

03 — Contaminate the imagery

Stock is fine. Untreated stock is not. Push every image through the same duotone or gradient map at partial strength, so a trace of your palette sits underneath the photograph. This is the single highest-yield move on this list, and it takes about forty seconds per image once the effect is saved.

The effect does not need to be strong. It needs to be consistent, because consistency across a set is what a template cannot fake for you.

04 — Break one frame

Let exactly one element cross a boundary it should respect. A photograph that runs past the column, a heading that overlaps the image, a rule that extends past the margin. One per layout. Two starts to look like an accident, and three looks like a different, worse template.

05 — Give the type somewhere to sit

Floating text over an image is the default because it is the easiest thing the software will let you do. Anchor it instead. A hard-edged block behind the caption, a drawn rule underneath a heading, a solid panel the text sits inside. Drawn edges beat implied ones, and they photograph better at thumbnail size, which is where most of your work will actually be seen.

06 — Delete one thing

Every template contains an element that exists to fill space in the demo. An icon row, a divider flourish, a decorative shape behind the headline. Find it and remove it. The layout gets quieter, and quiet is the least common quality in a feed.

The five-minute pass

Margins, type pairing, image treatment, one broken frame, one anchored caption, one deletion. Six moves, roughly five minutes, and the result no longer belongs to the fourteen thousand other people who downloaded the same file.

Why this works when taste does not

People assume the gap between their layouts and professional ones is aesthetic judgement, accumulated over years. Some of it is. Most of it is that a professional has made forty small decisions and an amateur has accepted forty defaults, and the reader is responding to the evidence of decision-making rather than to the decisions themselves.

You cannot acquire taste this afternoon. You can make six decisions this afternoon.
